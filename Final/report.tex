\section{Introduction}
The objective of this project was to implement and evaluate two fundamental Reinforcement Learning (RL) algorithms—State-Action-Reward-State-Action (SARSA) and Q-Learning—within the context of a 2D motion planning problem. The primary objective is to apply these algorithms to a 2D grid environment to find optimal paths from a starting position to a target while avoiding obstacles. This report details the map abstraction process, reward strategy design, and a comprehensive performance analysis based on various hyperparameters.

Our problem involves an agent navigating discrete map abstractions derived from BMP images. The agent must learn an optimal policy $\pi(s)$ via trial and error, relying solely on environmental rewards. We investigate how varying hyperparameters—such as exploration rate ($\epsilon$), discount factor ($\gamma$), and reward formulation—affect the efficiency and accuracy of both SARSA and Q-Learning agents.

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Map Abstraction}
Motion planning often requires reducing complex continuous or high-resolution spaces into manageable discrete states. In this project, given BMP maps were loaded as 2D NumPy arrays and abstracted. To prevent the state space from growing intractably large, a pooling-like mechanism is used to over-approximate obstacles. The high-resolution matrices were downscaled to fixed grid dimensions (e.g., $20 \times 20$ for map 1, and $40 \times 40$ for larger maps). During abstraction, any grid block containing a black pixel was marked entirely as an obstacle to ensure conservative pathfinding and collision avoidance.

\subsection{Environment and Agent}
The environment simulates the 2D map, permitting four discrete actions: \textit{Up, Down, Left,} and \textit{Right}. The agent utilizes a 3D Q-table (dimensioned by $x$-coordinate, $y$-coordinate, and action space) to track the expected utility of state-action pairs. During training, the agent employs an $\epsilon$-greedy strategy to balance exploration of new paths with exploitation of known optimal actions.

\subsection{Reward Strategies}
Two reward strategies were implemented:

\subsubsection{Strategy S1 (Naive)}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Reaching target: +100
    \item Hitting obstacle or boundary: -100
    \item Otherwise: -1 per step
\end{itemize}

This strategy encourages reaching the goal quickly while heavily penalizing invalid moves.

\subsubsection{Strategy S2 (Shaped Reward)}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Reaching target: +100
    \item Hitting obstacle: -100
    \item Step penalty: -1
    \item Additional reward based on Manhattan distance improvement:
    \[
    R += 10 \cdot (d_{\text{old}} - d_{\text{new}})
    \]
\end{itemize}

This provides more informative feedback, guiding the agent toward the goal.

\subsection{Learning Algorithms}
\subsubsection{Q-Learning}
\[
Q(s,a) \leftarrow Q(s,a) + \alpha \left[r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s',a') - Q(s,a)\right]
\]

\subsubsection{SARSA}
\[
Q(s,a) \leftarrow Q(s,a) + \alpha \left[r + \gamma Q(s',a') - Q(s,a)\right]
\]

\subsection{Visual Analysis of Advanced Environments}

The generated path plots for the advanced environments (Maze 1 and U-Map) provide crucial visual evidence that supports the quantitative results observed in our experiments. 

In the complex \textbf{Maze 1} environment (Figure 4), the limitations of the naive reward strategy (S1) are clearly visible. Under S1, SARSA struggles to navigate the deep corridors, often resulting in erratic or incomplete paths, which aligns with its low 21.86\% accuracy even with random starts. However, when the shaped reward strategy (S2) is introduced, the paths become noticeably more structured and goal-oriented. The Manhattan distance heuristic provides the necessary dense feedback to guide the agents through the maze's twists, visually corroborating the substantial jump in SARSA's accuracy to 75.44\%. Q-Learning, benefiting from its robust off-policy updates, manages to find highly efficient paths under both strategies, as seen in Figures 4a and 4b, matching its perfect 100\% accuracy in random start evaluations.

The \textbf{U-Map} environment (Figure 5) serves as a deceptive trap designed to exploit greedy pathfinding. The visual results distinctly illustrate why the S2 shaped reward strategy unexpectedly reduced accuracy during fixed-start training (dropping from $\sim$70\% to $\sim$46\% for both algorithms). Because S2 heavily rewards minimizing the Manhattan distance to the target, the agents are visually drawn directly into the inner dead-end of the "U" shape. The plots for the S2 strategy (Figures 5b and 5d) reveal how the agents take the most direct spatial route, only to become trapped by the surrounding walls, requiring extensive exploration to escape. In contrast, under the S1 naive strategy (Figures 5a and 5c), the lack of a distance-based incentive allows the agents to explore more broadly without being overwhelmingly lured into the dead-end trap, explaining the higher fixed-start accuracy for S1. When random starts are applied, the agents learn to navigate around the exterior of the U-Map from various angles, ultimately allowing them to solve the environment perfectly regardless of the trap.

\section{Conclusion}
This project successfully implemented and evaluated two foundational reinforcement learning algorithms, SARSA and Q-Learning, for 2D motion planning across various abstracted grid environments. Through comprehensive experimentation involving hyperparameter tuning, reward shaping, and varying map complexities, several key insights into the behavior and efficacy of these algorithms were obtained.

First, the method of initializing the agent's position during training plays a critical role in policy robustness. While a fixed starting position often leads to overfitting and poor generalization—causing the agents, particularly SARSA, to fail on complex topologies—randomized starting positions inherently force exploration across the entire state space. This drastically improves the accuracy and reliability of the resulting policies, enabling the agents to navigate around obstacles from any point on the map.

Second, the exploration-exploitation tradeoff ($\epsilon$) and the discount factor ($\gamma$) must be carefully tuned to the environment's complexity. We observed that extreme exploration ($\epsilon=1.0$) prevents on-policy algorithms like SARSA from converging, as the constant random actions frequently lead to failure states. Conversely, Q-Learning's off-policy nature—updating based on the maximum future expected reward regardless of the action taken—makes it significantly more resilient to high exploration rates. Furthermore, solving deep, complex mazes necessitates a high discount factor (e.g., $\gamma=1.0$) to ensure the agent values the long-term, delayed reward of reaching the target over immediate, short-sighted penalizations.

Third, the design of the reward function is paramount. While a naive reward strategy (S1) with sparse goal rewards and constant step penalties is sufficient for simple, obstacle-free maps, it struggles in deceptive environments like the "U-Map" or intricate mazes. By introducing a shaped reward strategy (S2) that incorporates Manhattan distance heuristics, we provided the agents with dense, informative feedback. This shaped reward effectively guided the agents out of local optima and dead ends, significantly boosting the accuracy of both algorithms and allowing SARSA to bridge the performance gap with Q-Learning.

Overall, Q-Learning demonstrated superior robustness and performance across the majority of our tests. Its ability to decouple the learned optimal policy from the exploratory behavior used during training makes it highly suitable for discrete 2D pathfinding. However, with appropriate reward shaping and randomized state initialization, SARSA also proved capable of learning highly efficient, conservative policies. Ultimately, this project highlights that while the underlying RL algorithms are powerful, their success in complex motion planning is heavily dependent on thoughtful environment abstraction, strategic reward design, and meticulous hyperparameter optimization.
